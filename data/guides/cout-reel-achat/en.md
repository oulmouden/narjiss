## The asking price is never the budget

This is every buyer's first bad surprise, and it is avoidable. A property price is a sale price: it does not include the deed costs, the duties owed to the State, the costs tied to a mortgage, or what you will have to spend before the home is liveable.

Depending on the type of property and how you finance it, the gap between the advertised price and the real budget is far from trivial. That is why our advisers always quote **two figures**: the price, and the budget to plan for.

!> Have the notary validate the average percentage to quote, first for a cash purchase, then for a mortgaged one. Publish nothing until that figure is confirmed.

## Costs tied to the deed of sale

These are unavoidable: without them, ownership is not transferred.

| Item | Paid to | When |
|---|---|---|
| Registration duties | Tax administration | On signature of the final deed |
| Land registry inscription | National land registry agency | When the file is filed |
| Certificate of ownership | Land registry | After inscription |
| Notary or adoul fees, plus VAT | The person drafting the deed | On signature |
| File and copy charges | The person drafting the deed | On signature |

!> Registration duty rate, land registry inscription fee and notary fee scale: to be confirmed by the notary, stating whether a special regime applies to new-build or social housing. These values change with the finance act.

## Costs tied to the mortgage

They only concern buyers who borrow, but for them they are systematic.

- **Bank arrangement fees**, taken when the loan is released.
- **Mortgage inscription** at the land registry, calculated on the amount borrowed.
- **Death and disability insurance**, obligatory in practice, priced according to your age and health.
- **Valuation of the property** by the bank, in some cases.

> These costs are negotiable, and they are negotiated before the agreement in principle, not after. Playing two banks against each other on arrangement fees pays for itself in a single meeting.

## What the price does not cover either

A newly delivered home is not a liveable home. Depending on the level of finish set out in the contract, plan for:

- the fitted kitchen, rarely included;
- light fittings, curtain rails, sometimes interior doors;
- connection and commissioning of the water and electricity meters;
- furniture, obviously, but also air conditioning if the project does not provide it.

And once you move in, recurring charges begin: building management, local services tax, housing tax where applicable, home insurance.

!> Check with the developer the exact level of finish delivered on each project, and have an accountant confirm which local taxes apply, with any exemptions for new-build.

## When each sum falls due

The order matters as much as the amount: many buyers have the funds, but not at the right moment.

1. **On reservation** — a deposit, whose amount and refund conditions are set out in the reservation contract.
2. **At each stage payment**, for an off-plan purchase — instalments tied to construction progress.
3. **On signature of the final deed** — the balance of the price, plus all deed costs.
4. **On handover** — connections, finishing works, fitting out.

## Three habits to adopt before signing

**Ask for the full budget in writing.** A serious adviser gives you a detailed item-by-item estimate, not a round number.

**Do your sums on the budget, never on the price.** The total budget is what determines whether the deal holds, and it is what you should compare between two properties.

**Keep a reserve.** An unexpected site delay, a slow loan release, a finish to be redone: a margin stops you having to make rushed decisions.
